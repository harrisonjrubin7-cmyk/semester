# Who owns this, and what it can be called

Two questions, both open, both cheap to close, and both blocking the same
thing: you cannot honestly raise money on a company whose ownership and whose
name are assumptions.

Neither is a code item. This file is here rather than in the project folder
because `ACTION-PLAN.md` items 3 and 10 are tracked here and kept going stale
there, and because the facts that decide question one are facts about how this
repository was built — which is a thing this repository can answer.

**Neither section below is legal advice.** Both are the preparation that makes
twenty minutes with somebody qualified productive instead of exploratory.

---

## 1 · Does Vanderbilt have a claim on Semester?

### What the policy actually says

Vanderbilt's Technology Policy applies to faculty, staff and students alike,
graduate and undergraduate. The default for a student is favourable and it is
stated plainly:

- **Students wholly own work product created in the classroom**, including new
  technology — inventions and software products — with limited exceptions.
- **Authored works are generally owned by their author**, with no ownership
  vesting in the university.
- Vanderbilt **does not claim** ideas created in common spaces on campus, nor
  ideas created using commonly available resources — common computers, library
  systems, general equipment or supplies.

So the starting position is that this is yours. The question is entirely about
the exceptions, and there are three that actually bite:

1. **Significant use of University resources.** The phrase is doing real work
   and it is not defined by a list. Commonly available resources are explicitly
   excluded; dedicated lab space, specialised equipment, or staff time are the
   shape of things that are not.
2. **Funding.** This is the one most likely to catch a student founder, and the
   policy is explicit about the consequence: *if the funding is determined to be
   a significant University resource, then Vanderbilt would own the resulting
   technology, and the student recipients would forego ownership.* A pitch
   competition prize, an accelerator stipend, a grant administered by the
   university — any of these can be that funding.
3. **University-supported or externally-supported research.** Work done as part
   of a research team, particularly under a federal grant.

### The facts that decide it — answer these before you write

The email below is much stronger if it states facts rather than asks an open
question. Work out your answer to each of these first:

- [ ] Has Semester ever received **money** through Vanderbilt — prize, stipend,
      grant, accelerator, course fund, anything administered by the university?
      Amount, source, date.
- [ ] Was any of it built **for course credit**? Which course, which term, and
      did a syllabus or a faculty member specify the deliverable?
- [ ] Has a **faculty or staff member** contributed to the design or the code,
      or supervised it as research?
- [ ] Was anything built on **university equipment or accounts** beyond a
      personal laptop, campus wifi and commonly available software?
- [ ] Are you in any **program** — accelerator, incubator, entrepreneurship
      course — whose participation agreement you signed? Those agreements can
      say things the Technology Policy does not.

If every answer is "no", you are almost certainly clear, and the email becomes
a request to have that confirmed in writing rather than a question. **Get it in
writing either way.** "We looked at the policy and we think we're fine" is not
something a diligence process accepts, and the cost of finding out otherwise
after a raise is not a cost you can absorb.

### The email

Ready to send. Fill the bracketed fields, delete this line and the heading
above it. CTTC is the right office; if they redirect you, the Wond'ry and the
entrepreneurship office are the usual second stops.

>

>
> The facts, as completely as I can state them:

>   names], outside of any course, on my own hardware.
> - It has received **no** university funding — no prize, stipend, grant or
>   administered award. [If untrue: "It received [amount] from [source] on
>   [date], and I would like to know whether that counts as a significant
>   University resource."]
> - No faculty or staff member has contributed to it or supervised it as
>   research.
> - It was not created for course credit and no syllabus specified it.
> - The only university resources involved are ones I understand to be commonly
>   available: campus wifi and my own laptop.
>
> My reading of the Technology Policy is that this falls squarely within
> student-owned work product, and that none of the exceptions — significant use
> of University resources, university-administered funding, or
> university-supported research — apply. I would be grateful if you could
> confirm that, or tell me what else you need in order to.
>
> I'm asking now rather than later because I expect to be talking to investors
> and I would rather have this settled in writing than assume it. If a short
> meeting is easier than email, I'm happy to come to you.
>
> Thank you,
> [name]
> [email] · [phone]

Two notes on the draft. It states facts and asks for confirmation rather than
asking "do you own my app?" — the second invites a cautious answer and starts a
review you did not need. And it says why you are asking, because a student who
mentions investors gets routed to the people who handle that, which is what you
want.

## 2 · The name

### The finding, and it is not a collision

I could not reach the USPTO databases from the environment this was written in
— `tmsearch.uspto.gov`, Justia and Trademarkia are all blocked by the network
policy here, and I am not going to report a clearance search I did not run.
**The search below is still yours to run**, and it takes about fifteen minutes.

But the collision question is the second question, and it is not the one that
decides this. The first one is whether "Semester" is registrable at all, and
the answer is very likely **no, not on the Principal Register, not as it
stands.**

A mark that is *merely descriptive* of the goods or services is refused
registration under **15 U.S.C. §1052(e)(1)**. "Semester" for software that
plans your semester describes the subject matter of the product about as
directly as a word can. This is not a close call and it does not depend on what
else is registered — an examining attorney can refuse it with no prior mark in
sight. Worse is possible: for a product whose whole function is organising a
semester, an examiner may treat the term as **generic** for the category, and a
generic term cannot be registered at all, ever, by anyone.

That is the finding. It is more actionable than a collision list, because it
holds regardless of what the search turns up.

### What that leaves you

| Route | What you get | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Supplemental Register** | Available for descriptive-but-not-generic marks. You may use ®, and later applicants for confusingly similar marks can be refused over it. | No presumption of validity, no incontestability, no nationwide constructive notice. Weak, but real, and a fallback an examiner may offer after a §2(e)(1) refusal. |
| **§2(f) acquired distinctiveness** | The Principal Register, eventually. | Five years of substantially exclusive and continuous use, or direct evidence of secondary meaning. You are a semester in. This is a 2031 route, not a 2026 one. |
| **A composite mark** | Register the word *with* a distinctive logo, and the registration protects the composite. | The word alone stays unprotected, and a competitor can use "semester" descriptively. Often the pragmatic answer. |
| **A distinctive house brand** | A coined or arbitrary word is registrable immediately and strongly. | You change the name, which costs you whatever recognition you have. Cheapest now, most expensive later. |

**The recommendation:** do not spend money filing "Semester" alone as a word
mark this semester. Either add a distinctive element and file the composite, or
treat the product name as a descriptive label and pick a protectable house mark
to sit above it. Decide before a fundraise, because "our name is unregistrable"
is a question you want to have already answered rather than discover in
diligence.

### The search to run anyway

Descriptiveness decides what you can register. A search decides whether you are
*infringing*, which is a different and more urgent risk — the Syllabuddy
example in the competitive notes is a name collision doing commercial damage
with no registration involved at all.

1. **USPTO** — `tmsearch.uspto.gov`. Search `semester` across **Class 9**
   (downloadable software) and **Class 42** (SaaS). Read live marks, and read
   the goods-and-services text rather than the mark alone: a live registration
   for "SEMESTER" on unrelated goods is not your problem, and a live
   application for something close in Class 42 is.
2. **Common-law use**, which the register does not show and which can still
   stop you: app store search on both stores, `semester` + `app`, and a domain
   check. An unregistered senior user with real commercial use in your field
   has rights you can be liable to.
3. **Record what you find, with dates.** A dated search is evidence of good
   faith if this ever matters, and it is worthless a year later if nobody wrote
   down when it was run.

If anything in step 1 or 2 is close, that is the point to pay a trademark
attorney for an opinion rather than to reason about it further yourself. A
clearance opinion is cheap relative to a rebrand after launch.
