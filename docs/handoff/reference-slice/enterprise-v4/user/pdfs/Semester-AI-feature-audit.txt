The documented user-facing behavior is straightforward: read a PDF, ask about confusing
content, or request a summary. The listing does not explain what happens internally.
Here is an illustrative soware-engineering study session—not a tested walkthrough of the app:
1. Open a PDF about database transactions.
2. Ask: “Explain atomicity using the examples in this document.”
3. Follow up: “How is atomicity different from isolation?”
4. Request: “Summarize the main points in five bullets.”
5. Compare the explanation with the relevant passages in the PDF.

Those prompts illustrate the advertised explanation and summarization functions. They do not
establish that the app can return accurate page citations, interpret diagrams, or restrict every
answer to the uploaded document.
The important distinction is between an advertised reading companion and a verified source-
grounded assistant. Semester AI advertises the former; its public listing does not document the
citation or grounding controls needed to establish the latter.
Semester AI specifically advertises capturing notes and having AI transform them into
organized digital content, followed by editing, enhancement, and annotation.
A useful acceptance-test workflow would be:
1. Capture a clearly written page of notes.
2. Inspect the resulting digital content for omissions.
3. Check headings, numbers, equations, and unusual terminology.
4. Edit an error and confirm the correction persists.
5. Add an annotation.
6. Verify backup, synchronization, and sharing separately.
That is a proposed test procedure—not a claim that each step has been independently verified.

most explicit documentation among these three. Semester AIʼs advertised distinction is
combining scanning and reading with course communities and human tutors—but its quiz
functionality and several implementation details still require verification.
